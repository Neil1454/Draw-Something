# Neil & Teri's Time Killer Games

*Ideal for those long flights where internet is miles away.* ✈️

*Created by Neil Sampson.*

Neil & Teri's Time Killer Games is nine two-player games for Android phones and tablets, played directly between your two devices.
**It works with no internet at all**, so you can play on a plane, because the devices talk to
each other over Bluetooth and/or Wi-Fi. After you pair, the host picks any game from the lobby,
and you can switch games without reconnecting.

### The new board games

**Draughts** follows the English rules: men move diagonally forward, kings one step in any direction, every capture is compulsory,
you must keep jumping with the same piece, and a man that reaches the far row becomes a king and stops there. The game is a draw after
80 half-moves with no capture and no man moved.

**Ludo** (Frustration) has two players on the classic cross board, four pieces each. A 6 brings a piece out and gives another go (three 6s
in a row lose the turn). Landing on a lone enemy piece sends it back to base unless it sits on a start square or a star. To get home you
need the exact number, so a roll that is too big can't be used by that piece.

**Monopoly** uses the UK London board (Old Kent Road to Mayfair, four stations, two utilities), £1500 each, £200 for GO, £50 jail fine.

Building is part of the play: squares you can build on glow green with a bobbing 🔨 (🏨 when the next step is a hotel); tap one to see its houses, the next rent, and exactly why you can't build if you can't (need the whole set, build evenly, cash, mortgage). Squares you still need to finish a set glow gold 🎯 (🔒 red if the other player owns them; toggle with "Set hints"). A coach tip says what to do next, and a banner celebrates completing a colour set.

Ownership is easy to see: an owned square is washed in its owner's colour with a thick border and a badge showing their token (🎩 or 🚗), and a **Who owns what** panel lists every property by colour set (★ SET when someone has the lot). Events are lively: coins fly between players on rent, tax and purchases, money floats up, a claim burst appears when a square is bought, confetti on passing GO, flashing jail, a headline banner for each event, and chasing lights on the MONOPOLY sign.
Rent doubles on a complete unbuilt set, houses must be built evenly, hotels need four houses first, and the bank has 32 houses and 12 hotels.
The game opens with a roll to see who goes first (highest total, ties re-rolled). **House rule (on by default, switch off in the lobby):** you
must go round the board once, collecting from GO, before you can buy; the official rulebook lets you buy from your first go. Unwanted
properties go to a two-player auction (either player can bid, from £1). You can mortgage (and lift it for the loan plus 10%), sell buildings back for half
price, and trade properties and cash (whoever receives a mortgaged property pays the bank 10% interest straight away, as in the official rules). If you can't cover a bill you must raise the money or go bankrupt. The 16 Chance and 16 Community
Chest cards follow the UK edition (the wording is paraphrased). Choose a short game and the richest player wins after 15 or 30 turns each.
The computer plays all three, including bidding at auctions and judging trade offers.

### Centipede extras

Hit things in quick succession and your **combo** climbs (x2 at 4 hits, up to x5), multiplying every point. If you and your partner
land hits within a moment of each other you get a **TEAM COMBO** bonus. Killed heads (and sometimes other segments) drop pick-ups:
rapid fire, triple shot, an extra life, or a **blast** that blows up the six segments nearest you. When only three segments are left
the centipede goes into a red **FRENZY** and speeds up. The play area flashes red when it gets close, and floating words pop up for every moment.

### Stakes (play for something)

In the lobby, before any game, each of you can set **stakes**: money (just type it, £5), a treat, a chore, anything. You can add up to five,
and each one is either **shown** to the other player right away or kept a **mystery** (its words are never sent until the game ends).
The 🏆 button in every game shows what's at stake, and when the game ends a banner says who claims what (mystery stakes get revealed).
The app never handles money: a stake is just a promise between the two of you.

### Stakes Showdown

A game made for stakes. Every stake you each put up is its own contest. The next stake is turned over (mystery ones flip face-up), and
the player who did **not** put it up is the challenger and picks the contest from four options. Each option says what you need to do to
win (for example "Connect four discs in a row before your rival does"). The contest is a real game from the app: Connect 4, Pairs, Quick
Draw, Pig, Othello, Higher or Lower, Blackjack, Minesweeper, Battleships, Space Invaders, Centipede or Arkanoid. Win it and you claim the
stake. A tie **rolls the stake over** into the next round and the winner takes both. At the end, whoever claimed the most wins (money
amounts break a tie). You each need at least one stake, and it needs two real players (no computer opponent).

### Mancala

Six pits and a store each, four seeds (or 3 or 6) in every pit. Tap one of your pits to pick up its seeds and sow them counter-clockwise, one per pit, through
your store but past your rival's. **Last seed in your store: go again.** **Last seed in an empty pit of yours with seeds opposite: capture them.** When a
side runs out, the other player keeps what is left on their side and the bigger store wins. The seeds drop one at a time with a sound each, and the computer
player searches ahead (a little on Easy, deep on Hard).

### Liar's Dice

Three or five dice each (the host chooses), optionally with ones wild. Each phone rolls its own dice and they never leave it until somebody calls liar.
Take turns bidding on all the dice on the table ("four 5s"), each bid higher than the last, or call **LIAR!** Both hands are revealed with the matching
dice lit up: if there were at least as many as the bid, the caller loses a die, otherwise the bidder does. The loser opens the next round, and when you lose
your last die you're out. The computer player works out the odds of each bid, and bluffs now and then.

### Guess Who?

Each player secretly picks one card from the same board. The host chooses **what is on the cards** (people, animals, wild animals, farm and pets, birds and sea life,
people and animals mixed, everyday items, or everything) and **how many** (12, 24, 36 or 48; big boards use six columns). Every card differs from every other in
at least a few ways: the 48 people vary by hair colour and style, eyes, glasses, hats, beards, moustaches, big noses, rosy cheeks, freckles, bushy eyebrows,
big smiles, earrings, bow ties and scarves; animals and items have their own traits. Only questions that would actually split the board are listed. As in the
real game, you ask yes/no questions and flip down what can't be it. Take turns asking a question and **the other player answers
it themselves**: the question pops up with a big card showing their own face, and they tap **Yes** or **No**. The app makes sure the answers to the listed
questions are true (a wrong tap shakes the card and asks you to look again), so nobody can fib their way to a win. You can also **type your own question**
("Do they look friendly?"), which is answered on trust.

The question list is grouped by topic (hair, eyes, face, clothes for people; what it is, where it lives, how it looks for animals; and so on), with big picture chips. With the **question helper** on (a lobby
option), every chip shows how many of the faces still in play would be Yes and No, and a **💡 Best question** button picks the one that splits them most
evenly. Asked questions show their answers, and the whole conversation appears as chat bubbles. Tap faces to flip them down (or turn on **auto-flip**, and the
faces your answers rule out flip for you); a counter shows how many faces are left. When you're sure, make a guess: right wins, wrong loses on the spot.
Each player's secret stays on their own phone until the game ends. The lobby also lets you switch off hand answers (questions are then answered automatically).

### Noughts and Crosses, Hangman, Air Hockey, Trivia and Deal or No Deal

**Noughts and Crosses** has two versions. *Classic* is the usual 3 x 3 grid. *Ultimate* is nine small grids making one big grid: the square you play
in decides which small grid your rival must play in next (if that grid is already finished they can play anywhere), you claim a small grid by getting three
in a row inside it, and you win by claiming three in a row on the big grid. The host can play one game or a best of three or five; the player who goes first swaps each game.

**Hangman** has two ways to play. In *Duel* you take turns being the setter: the setter secretly types a word (letters only, 3 to 14) and an optional hint, and the
other player guesses letters or risks the whole word (a wrong word guess costs two lives). In *Shared* the app picks a random word and you take turns on one board: a
right letter lets you go again, a wrong one costs you both a life and passes the turn. Eight wrong guesses and the stick figure is hanged. Solving a word scores 100 minus
10 per mistake (never under 20); a word that stumps the guesser scores 60 for the setter. The secret word stays on the setter's phone until the round is over.

**Air Hockey and Pong** is a real-time game where the host's phone runs the table. You are always at the bottom of your own screen (the guest's picture is
turned round). In *Air Hockey* the mallet follows your finger on the pad under the table (two-way, within your own half) and the puck bounces off the walls into goals; a puck
jammed in a corner is put back in the middle. In *Pong* your paddle slides along your end and the ball speeds up with every hit. First to 5, 7 or 10 wins.

**Trivia** uses the same quiz engine as *Do you know your Holiday?* (first correct answer scores most, speed bonus, "give me more time", a bonus round for the
questions you missed) with general knowledge topics. Each topic is a JSON file in `app/src/main/assets/trivia/`, in the same format as the holiday packs; drop another file in
to add a topic.

**Deal or No Deal** follows the UK show: 22 boxes from 1p to £250,000. You each keep one box, then take turns opening the other twenty (4, 4, 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 1 per
round). After each round the Banker offers each of you money (less than the average of the prizes left, rising towards it as the game goes on, with a bit of mood). You each
secretly choose DEAL or NO DEAL and the answers are revealed together; a player who deals leaves the game and their box is opened. If you are both still in at the end, you may swap
boxes, but only if you both agree. The most money wins. The prizes in the boxes are never sent to the other phone until they are opened.

**Deal or No Deal** has been given the full studio treatment: lit prize boards, red 3D boxes, and every box you choose or open comes up in the middle of the screen,
shakes with a drum roll and bursts open in a flash of light as the prize card rises out and counts up (blue sparkles for a small prize, a gasp for a big one; tap to skip).
After each round the phone rings, and rings, until you pick it up; then the Banker's tune plays, a drum roll builds and his offer lights up before you choose DEAL or NO DEAL. The
tune is an original piece in the same spirit, not the television theme (which is copyrighted). While the Banker talks you hear a muffled voice down a bad phone line, the boxes knock and creak
before they burst open, a crowd applauds a small prize or groans at a big one, big words appear on screen ("OUCH! A BIG ONE GONE!"), the box numbers are always shown, and a quiet, tense game-show
tune plays underneath. None of it is a recording: the sounds are built in code (`Studio.kt`) the way real ones are made, from struck-metal bells, plucked strings, brass, drums, a hundred separate claps
for applause and a buzzing "throat" run through the formants of a mouth for voices, with a little room reverb. All the game's sounds were re-made this way, so nothing sounds like an old games console any more.

**Slot Machine** is shared: you take turns on the same five-reel, three-row machine with nine paylines (across, V, upside-down V, two zig-zags and two bumps), 1,000 credits
each. You choose a bet per line (1, 2, 5 or 10; a spin costs nine lines' worth) and pull the lever, and the host's phone does all the random drawing so nobody can cheat.
Symbols pay from the left: cherries 4/12/40 for 3/4/5 up to sevens 50/150/500 (five 7s, or five wilds, is the jackpot); a pair of cherries pays a little; ⭐ wilds stand in for any
symbol; three or more 💰 scatters pay a prize and give 8, 12 or 20 free spins in which every win is doubled. After any win you can collect it or gamble it on a hidden red or
black card (up to three doublings in a row). The machine now looks and works like a proper casino machine: a gold cabinet with chasing bulbs, a **pay table across the top** (the symbols that just paid light up),
coloured **payline numbers** down both sides, a **pull-down lever** with a red ball (tap it or drag it down), glowing red **digital meters** (credits, bet per line, total bet, win), a **coin tray**
that fills with your credits and rattles coins in when you win, and a deck of bevelled **gold buttons**: BET (cycles your bet), MAX BET (biggest bet and spin), a big red SPIN, COLLECT, and the red and black GAMBLE buttons. On its side the machine fills the screen, with the meters and buttons in a column beside it. The reels whirr with motion blur and bounce as they land, the winning
symbols pop and twinkle while each winning line draws itself across from the left, the bulbs chase slowly when idle, faster while spinning and flash when you win, a glint sweeps across the glass, the win meter counts up,
and when the first three reels already show two scatters the last two creep to a stop inside a flashing frame (with a rising sound) in case a third turns up. The SPIN button turns into STOP while the reels are going, to stop them early. The machine returns about 91% of what is staked in the long run, and a test checks that. The reels whirr and stop one by one with a bounce,
winning lines light up in different colours, and big wins and jackpots rain coins.

**A place for every game.** The games no longer all share one purple background. Each is played somewhere of its own, drawn in code and gently animated: Pig on a green felt table
under a swaying lamp, Draughts, Othello and Ludo on a wooden table top, Battleships out at sea, Minesweeper on a radar screen, Quick Draw in a neon arcade, Pairs in a playroom, Connect 4 in arcade blue,
the holiday quiz on a beach at sunset, Trivia in a television studio with sweeping spotlights, and Pictionary on a slate of chalk doodles. Score popups now slam in with a burst of light, and big moments
get a **big word across the screen** ("CAPTURED!", "👑 KINGED!", "CORNER TAKEN!", "SENT HOME!") and a reaction from the crowd; every game ends with the crowd cheering, clapping or groaning.

**Title cards.** Every game now opens with its own title card (its picture, name and a line about it on the game's colours) and its own short jingle, so each one starts with a character of its own.

### Blackjack

You both play against the dealer at the same time with £500 each, for 5, 10 or 15 rounds. Pick your chips and press **Deal**; then **Hit**,
**Stand** or **Double** (first two cards only). Blackjack pays 3 to 2, a tie gives your bet back, and the dealer stands on every 17.
The most chips at the end wins; if someone is out of chips the game ends early. The dealer's face-down card is never sent to the
guest's phone until it is turned over.

**Coach mode** is chosen by the host before starting: **Off**, **3 tips each**, **5 tips each** or **Unlimited**. When on, every hand shows
your bust chance, and **Ask for a tip** (one per use from your allowance) says what basic strategy would do and why, and makes that
button glow. Tips are private: your partner's allowance and tips are their own.

### The control pad (Arkanoid, Space Invaders, Centipede)

In the games where you move something along the bottom with your finger, there is now a marked **control pad under the screen**: put your finger anywhere
on it and slide, and your paddle or ship follows your finger's position across the pad. Your hand never covers the playing area. Holding a finger
down on the pad also fires (and, in Arkanoid, fires the lasers). The pad is low and slim so the game gets the room. Your ship's position is shown on it, and a short "SLIDE HERE" with arrows shows until you touch it; nothing else appears while you use it.

### Arkanoid

Slide your finger along the pad under the screen to move your paddle. Bricks come in colours (worth 50 to 120 by row, as in the arcade original), silver
(two hits, worth 50 times the round), steel (three hits, 80 times the round), gold (unbreakable) and red (explodes its neighbours).

The classic **power-up capsules** fall from broken bricks (catch them with a paddle; a new paddle power replaces the last one):
**E** expand · **L** lasers (keep your finger down) · **C** catch (a sticky paddle that holds the ball: lift your finger and tap the pad to let go) ·
**D** disruption (three balls) · **S** slow · **B** break (an exit opens on the right edge: slide your paddle off it to skip the round) ·
**P** player (an extra life for the team). Extras: **F** fireball, **×2** double points. Two bad ones have a flashing yellow warning edge:
**N** shrinks the paddle that catches it and **»** makes the ball much faster for a while.

**Enemies** (spinning orbs, cones and molecules) drift down the screen and knock the ball about: hit or shoot them for 100 points. The ball
**speeds up the longer a rally goes on** (up to 1.5 times, shown as SPEED at the top; dropping the ball resets it), and every level starts faster. On
the final round (when there are three or more rounds) you face **DOH**, a boss with a health bar who drops bombs: a bomb that lands on a paddle costs the team a
life. Lives are shared by the team; the higher score wins.

**Harder, if you want it.** The host can choose a **Small or Tiny paddle**, a **Fast or Frantic ball** (a higher starting speed that builds up quicker
during a rally), and a **Falling wall** that creeps down a row every 30, 24 or 18 seconds: if a brick gets pushed off the bottom row the team loses a life.
There are now **nine different walls** (including a diamond, stripes between gold pillars, an invader and a fortress), purple **mystery bricks** that always drop
a capsule, an **H shield** capsule (a glowing floor that saves the next dropped ball), and a nasty **⇄ reverse** capsule that flips the controls of whoever catches it.

### Vibration

Every game now buzzes with the action: each sound effect has a matching feel (a tick for a tap, a rumble for an explosion,
a rising buzz for a win). Switch it off with **Vibration (all games)** in Settings. It uses Android's "vibrate" permission, which needs no prompt.

### Space Invaders and Centipede

Both are real-time, two players on the same screen. Slide your finger anywhere to fly your ship and hold it down to fire (you can rest your
thumb wherever is comfortable: it moves your ship by how far you slide it). The host's phone runs the whole game about 30 times a second and
sends the picture to the other phone; the other phone only sends where its finger is and whether it is firing, so there is nothing to cheat
with. Power-ups (rapid fire, triple shot, extra life) come from the mystery ship in Space Invaders and the spider in Centipede. A ship that
runs out of lives comes back when the wave or level is cleared, and the game ends when both are out, the invaders land, or all waves are done.
Both can be played portrait or landscape, and the playfield resizes to fit.

### Screen orientation

Games that suit it have a layout for each way up (Othello, Connect 4, Pairs, Ludo, Draughts, Tetris, Minesweeper, Battleships, Pig, the
holiday quiz and the arcade games), and resize as you turn the phone. **Monopoly is landscape only**: it turns the phone for you while you play.
Its board is much bigger, every square has its full name, and you can pinch to zoom, drag to move, and double-tap to zoom in and out.

### Saving long games

Monopoly, Ludo and Draughts can be saved and finished later.
- **Automatic.** While a long game is being played, the host's phone keeps a save up to date after every move, and writes it again the
  moment the app goes into the background or closes. If the app is closed, the phone runs out of battery or the app crashes, the game is
  still there (at worst the last second or so is missing). The save is written to a temporary file and then renamed, so a crash can't
  leave a half-written save.
- **Leaving.** Pressing ✕ or Back in a long game asks "Save your game?" with *Save and leave*, *Don't save* and *Keep playing*. *Don't save*
  asks a second time, because everything is lost for both players. The guest is simply told the host keeps the progress.
- **Resuming.** The host opens the app, pairs up with their partner the normal way (or plays the computer), opens the game's lobby and presses
  **Resume** on the green saved-game card (it shows the score, when it was saved and who it was with). The partner just joins as usual and
  sees the game continue exactly where it stopped, including a half-finished auction or a debt in Monopoly. You can also **Discard** it.
  Starting a new game while one is saved asks first, and a finished game removes its save automatically.
- **Where it lives.** Only on the host's phone, in the app's private storage (one file per game), and it is never sent anywhere except to
  your partner during the game. Damaged or tampered saves are checked and refused.

## Play solo, or learn by watching

- **🤖 Solo vs computer.** Press it on the home screen, choose Easy, Normal or Hard, then pick any game as
  usual. The computer is a stand-in for a second phone, so every game, rule and option works exactly as it
  does with a real partner. It only uses what you could see (it can't peek at hidden cards, ships or mines).
  In Pictionary it's practice only: you draw every round and the computer pretends to guess, because it
  can't read a drawing.
- **📖 Learn to play.** Pick a game and watch it play itself with captions explaining each step (and a few
  tips), instead of reading the rules. Pause, step through one caption at a time, or speed it up. Nothing
  needs connecting and nobody else needs to be there.

## Do you know your Holiday? (quiz)

A head-to-head quiz about where you're going. Both phones see the same question and four answers; the
**first correct answer** earns 100 points plus up to 100 for speed, a slower right answer still earns 40, and a
wrong answer costs 25. After each question the right answer and a "did you know" fact appear. The correct answer
is never sent to the other phone until it is revealed, and the answers are shuffled every time.

Questions can also be **pictures** (a flag, a clock, a price tag, a plug, a beach flag, a turtle, a kitesurfer, all drawn in code) and **maps**:
tap a dot on a map of the islands (or of Sal) to choose it, then tap it again or press "Lock in". Place names stay hidden until the answer is revealed.
Add `"art": "pic:flag"` (also `plug`, `clock:15:00`, `tag:330_CVE`, `turtle`, `kite`, `beachflag:red`) or `"art": "map:islands"` / `"map:sal"`
to a question; map questions list the places (up to 10) as their answers.

**Extra time.** While the clock is ticking you can press "Give me more time" (up to 3 times per quiz, once per question). You get
extra seconds (half the question time, at least 10), but a right answer is then worth a flat 35 points: no speed bonus and never the
"first right answer" bonus. So the points always rank in this order: first right answer (100 + speed) > slower right answer (40) > right
with extra time (35) > ran out of time (0) > wrong (-25).

**Bonus (redemption) round.** After the last question each player gets their own round made from the questions they got wrong or didn't
answer (up to 8, most valuable first, in a fresh answer order). A right answer wins back exactly the points you lost on that question
(never more, so wild guessing can't pay), or 10 for a question you didn't answer. Get every bonus question right for a +10
perfect-recall bonus. Nobody has to wait: the two rounds run side by side, and a player with nothing to redo just waits. The host can switch
the bonus round off in the lobby. The end screen shows main-round and bonus points separately.

There are two holidays so far: **Cape Verde · Sal** and **Tenerife**, both written for what a visitor will actually meet.

The first holiday is **Cape Verde · Sal**, focused on what a visitor will actually meet: money and prices, getting around, food and menus, beaches and sea safety,
excursions, hotels, language and people. The questions are written from general knowledge, so double-check
anything time-sensitive (entry rules, prices) before you travel.

### Adding another holiday
Drop a JSON file in `app/src/main/assets/holidays/`; the app finds it on start-up (it then shows up as a choice in the lobby).

```json
{
  "id": "tenerife",
  "title": "Tenerife",
  "emoji": "🌋",
  "blurb": "One line shown in the lobby.",
  "questions": [
    { "q": "Which volcano towers over Tenerife?", "a": ["Teide", "Etna", "Vesuvius", "Fuji"], "c": 0,
      "fact": "Shown after the answer.", "cat": "Nature" }
  ]
}
```
Each question needs 3 or 4 different answers; `c` is the position (from 0) of the right one. The app shuffles them.
Questions that don't make sense are skipped, and `HolidayTests` checks every bundled pack (at least 30 questions, four unique
answers, the right answer not always in the same slot or always the longest).

### Removing the feature
Set `Features.HOLIDAY` to `false` in `core/Holiday.kt` and the quiz disappears from every menu. To remove it for good, delete
`core/Holiday.kt`, `ui/HolidayScreens.kt`, `assets/holidays/` and the `HOLIDAY` lines the compiler then points at.

## Scores, leaderboards and the Dominator

Every finished game is recorded on both phones for both players (by name). **Scores** on the home screen shows a
leaderboard for each game (ranked by wins, then points) and the overall **Dominator** table: a win is worth 100, a draw 40,
a loss 10, plus up to 25 for the points you scored in the game. Games against an Easy computer, and Pictionary practice,
don't count; scores live only on the phone (and can be reset).

## Sound

Every sound effect is generated in code (`core/Synth.kt`), so there are no audio files. There is a mute button on the home screen.

## Tetris (falling blocks, head to head)

Tetris played side by side: you each play your own board, and each phone shows the other player's board live.
The whole screen is the control, there are no buttons:
* **Slide** your finger sideways: the piece follows. **Tap** the left half to turn left, the right half to turn right.
* **Drag down** and hold: soft drop. **Flick down** (long, fast, straight): hard drop. **Flick up**: hold.
* Mistake protection: a small wobble does nothing, a gesture stays sideways or vertical once recognised, and a hard drop needs a clear flick.
  Three touch feels (Relaxed / Normal / Quick) in ⚙ Settings.
* Settings (⚙ on the home screen and in-game): four block styles (Glass, Neon, Candy, Classic), vibration, optional quiet music (off by default).

Clear rows to send **gift pieces** to your rival. A charge meter builds with every clear (single 1, double 3, triple 5, four 10, plus combo and back-to-back
bonuses); every 2 charge becomes one gift, so even singles send a gift every other line.

How gifts work (modelled on how other versus Tetris games handle attacks, but with pieces you control instead of rising junk rows):
* Gifts wait in a **meter** for 3 seconds. Clearing 2+ rows in that time **cancels** them (oldest first), as in Tetris 99 and similar games.
* The rest join your **NEXT queue** as gold striped pieces. You place them yourself, at normal speed, with full control, like any other piece.
* They arrive **at most every other piece** and not straight after you clear a row, so you always get breathing room.
* Long no-clock matches speed up on their own after 5 minutes ("overtime"), so a match always ends.

Seven game modes:

| Mode | How it works |
|---|---|
| ⚔️ Battle | Last stack standing wins. |
| ⏱ Score attack | Best score when the 2, 3 or 5 minute clock runs out wins. Gifts optional. |
| 🏁 Sprint | First to clear 20, 40 or 60 lines wins. Gifts optional. |
| 💣 Bomb Blitz | Every 6th piece is a bomb. Its blast blows a hole, the blocks above fall in, and the blown-up blocks are sent to your rival as gifts. |
| 🌊 Rising Tide | A junk row with one gap rises from the bottom of both boards every 12 seconds (the same gap on both). |
| 🔥 Turbo | The speed goes up every 25 seconds whatever you do. |
| ⚡ Power Surge | Every 5th piece carries a star block. Clear a row with a star to launch a power: dizzy (rival's left and right swapped), turbo curse (they fall 3x faster), fog (they can't see ahead), double gift, or clean-up (your bottom rows vanish). |

Also: back-to-back four-line clears (x1.5 and an extra gift), perfect clears (empty board: big bonus and four gifts), combos, line-clear flashes,
bomb explosions, ghost piece, hold, next-three preview, wall kicks, soft and hard drop, swipe controls on your board (slide, swipe down, flick to drop),
Gentle / Fast / Frantic start speeds. Both players get exactly the same piece sequence (a shared 7-piece bag), so it is fair, and each phone
simulates its own board so the controls never lag; only the board picture, gift pieces and curses cross the link. It works against the
computer too (Easy / Normal / Hard, and it knows how to aim a bomb) and has a Learn demo.

## Minesweeper (minesweeper for two)

When a mine goes off the phone gives a short buzz (it uses the "vibrate" permission, which needs no prompt).

A shared grid (6×6, 8×8, 10×10 or 12×12). You **take turns** tapping a square; a number tells you how
many bombs touch it. Your first square is always safe.

- **Safe square:** +10 each. An empty square opens its neighbours too, so a lucky open scores a lot.
- **Bomb:** −25 (never below 0) and your turn ends. The game carries on.
- **Hidden power-ups** fire when you reveal them: 🎁 Bonus (+30), 🛡️ Shield (blocks your next bomb, +10),
  ✨ Double (your next reveal scores double), ⏩ Extra turn, 🧲 Steal (take 20 points from your opponent),
  📡 Radar (flags a hidden bomb for both of you).
- The game ends when every safe square is open. Highest score wins.

The host's phone holds the real minefield and referees every move; the other phone is only ever sent
what has been revealed, so there is nothing to peek at.

## Battleships (battleships)

Each player has a hidden fleet (4 ships on 8×8, 5 on 10×10) and **arranges their own fleet by moving the ships on screen**: your whole fleet starts out on your sea, so
**drag** a ship and it lifts off the water and follows your finger from exactly where you touched it; a green outline shows where it will land (it slides to the nearest
free spot if you drop it on another ship, and goes back where it came from only if nowhere nearby is free). **Tap** a ship (or its name in the fleet row under the board) to select it
(it glows gold); then **↻ Turn** it a quarter turn about its middle (a **double tap** on a ship does the same), fine-tune it with the **◀ ▲ ▼ ▶** buttons one square at a time, and **↶ Undo**
any move. **Shuffle** deals a new random layout. **Nothing is locked in until you press READY FOR BATTLE**: until then every ship can be moved and turned freely; after it, a padlock appears on each ship.
The ships
are drawn as real ships: carrier, battleship, cruiser, submarine and destroyer. The lobby has a **Random fleets** switch to
skip placing. Bombs drop in from the top of the screen with splashes, fire, smoke and sinking ships.
Take turns firing at the other player's grid:

- 🎯 **Shot:** one square. **A hit means you fire again.**
- 📡 **Radar (once):** counts the ship squares in a 3×3 area. Uses your turn.
- 💥 **Cluster bomb (once):** hits a plus-shaped area of 5 squares. Uses your turn.

Sink every enemy ship to win. Fleets never leave your phone: each phone answers the other's shots.

## How Draw Something works

1. **Everyone types their name** and one player taps **Host a game**. The other taps **Join a
   game**, picks the host from the list, and the host taps **Accept**. That's the whole setup.
2. Both players read and tap **I agree to the rules** (see below). Then the host picks how many
   rounds (4–10), how long each drawing gets (60/90/120 s) and the **word difficulty**:
   *Everything* (easy to hard, with the odd extra-hard one), *No easy words*, *Hard*, or
   *Extra hard* (scenes, actions and ideas such as "traffic jam" or "gravity").
3. You **take turns** drawing. The drawer sees five words (always a spread of difficulty) and can pick one
   or tap **🎲 Surprise me** and let the game choose.
4. The drawer draws; the guesser sees every stroke **as it's drawn**, with the category and a
   `_ _ _ _` letter mask as a hint.
5. The guesser types a guess and sends it. The drawer sees it and taps **✓ Correct** or
   **✗ Not quite**. (The game highlights an exact or near match to help, but the drawer decides.)
6. Points are awarded, roles swap, repeat. Highest total wins; **Play again** is one tap.

Drawing tools: 12 colours, 4 brush sizes, eraser, undo and clear. No fill bucket, no layers.

## Rules

Both players agree to these before the game starts (they're always one tap away under **📜 Rules**):

1. Drawing only. No letters, words or numbers, no labels or captions.
2. Don't spell it out: no initials, no first letter, no letters hidden in the drawing.
3. No spoken or acted-out hints.
4. Guessers: no peeking at the drawer's screen.
5. Be fair: accept right guesses, decline wrong ones, only report a genuine rule break.

**Hints (optional):** the host can switch hints on or off in the lobby before starting. When on, the guesser gets
3 hints per game. A hint shows some letters of the word (more each time) for 6 seconds, then hides again. Not in solo practice.

**Reporting a rule break:** if the drawer breaks a rule, the guesser taps **⚑ Rule break** and picks
what happened (text, label/caption, spelling it out, or hints). The drawer sees the report and
chooses **They're right** or **I disagree**.

- If the drawer agrees: they lose **20 points**, their drawing is **cleared** (so it has to be
  redrawn without the offending part) and any guess that was waiting is cancelled **without**
  costing the guesser anything.
- If the drawer disagrees: nobody loses points and play carries on.
- Each player can report at most **3 times per round**, one at a time, so reports can't be spammed.
- The clock doesn't stop for a report, and the round can't time out while one is unanswered.

## Scoring (designed to be fair to both players)

| | |
|---|---|
| Round value | Up to **100** points for an instant guess, falling smoothly to **30%** when the clock runs out |
| Who earns it | **Both players**, from the same clock, so a clear drawing and a sharp eye both pay, and neither gains by sabotaging the other |
| Difficulty | Easy ×1.0 · Medium ×1.25 · Hard ×1.5 · Extra hard ×2.0, applied to both players |
| Wrong guess | Guesser loses **5** for each guess the drawer rejects |
| Skipping a word | Drawer loses **15** per skip (you then pick a new word) |
| Rule break | Drawer loses **20** each time they agree to a report (more than a skip, because it's cheating) |
| Time runs out | Nobody scores (skips still cost the drawer); scores never go below 0 |
| Verdict delay | Time is stamped when the guess *arrives*, so a drawer who is slow to tap Correct never costs anyone points |

Roles alternate every round, so both players draw and guess equally often. Because both
players are paid from the same clock, the thing that separates them is their own accuracy:
fewer wrong guesses, skips and rule breaks. The numbers live in
[`Scoring.kt`](app/src/main/java/com/neil1454/drawsomething/core/Scoring.kt) if you want to tune them.

## How the devices connect

The app uses Google's **Nearby Connections** API (`play-services-nearby`). It chooses the best
available radio by itself (Bluetooth, BLE, Wi-Fi Direct / hotspot / same-network Wi-Fi) and
needs **no internet**, so it works in airplane mode as long as you switch Bluetooth (and/or
Wi-Fi) back on. The first time you host or join, Android asks for the Nearby/Bluetooth
permissions (on Android 11 and older that means Location, which must also be switched on).

Requirements: Android 8.0+ and Google Play services on both devices (standard on almost
every phone/tablet; Amazon Fire tablets don't have it).

## Security

- **Encrypted, authenticated link.** Nearby Connections encrypts everything between the two
  devices, and the connection is only made after a manual accept from **both** players.
- **Pairing code.** When two devices connect, both screens show the same 4-digit code derived from
  the encrypted handshake. Each player checks it matches before tapping **Codes match**. Someone
  who isn't your partner, or something pretending to be them, can't produce a matching code.
- **Door locked behind you.** The moment your partner is connected, the app stops advertising and
  searching, rejects every other connection attempt, and only reads data from the one paired device.
  While hosting, the host sees any extra requests (name + code) and can pick the right one or decline.
  People without the app can't see or join anything at all.
- **The internet is used for one thing only: updates.** The games never touch the network. The app
  does hold the INTERNET permission, but the only code that uses it is the **Check for updates**
  button on the home screen. Nothing runs in the background and nothing is sent when you play. When
  you tap it, the app asks GitHub for this project's latest release and, if you agree, downloads it.
  Every connection is HTTPS and only to GitHub's own hosts (also across redirects); the file must come
  from this repository's releases; its size and SHA-256 are checked; and before Android is asked to
  install it the app confirms it is the same app, newer, and signed by the same key as your copy.
  Android then still asks you to tap Install. The app keeps no account, location or contact data
  (only your name, saved on your phone), and cloud backup is switched off.
- **Defensive decoding.** Every incoming message is size-limited and range-checked (text length,
  coordinates, scores, number of strokes), so a damaged or hostile packet can't crash the app or use
  up memory. Unit tests cover this.
- **Be aware.** The pairing check is only as good as the players: always compare the code. While you
  are *hosting* (before your partner joins) your name is visible to other copies of this app nearby,
  so host just before pairing. Scores are worked out by the drawer's phone and trusted by the
  guesser's: fine for a friendly game, not cheat-proof.
- **Signing key.** Android only installs an update that is signed with the same key as the installed
  copy. By default builds use a convenience key committed to this repo (so anyone can build the
  project). That key is public, so for a real release a *private* key can be stored as GitHub secrets
  (`RELEASE_KEYSTORE_B64`, `RELEASE_KEYSTORE_PASSWORD`, `RELEASE_KEY_ALIAS`, `RELEASE_KEY_PASSWORD`).
  The build uses it automatically, and only for builds of `main`: branches and pull requests (including
  from forks) never see it. Switching keys means each phone must uninstall once, because Android treats it
  as a different publisher.
- **Release builds.** Released APKs are proper release builds (not debuggable), checked in CI to be signed
  with the intended key. The build robot has read-only access except in the one job that publishes a release.

## Updating

Tap **🔄 Check for updates** on the home screen. The app looks at the latest GitHub release and, if it
is newer, offers to download and install it. The first time, Android asks you to allow this app to
install updates (Settings → “Allow from this source”). It works without any login or key as long as
the GitHub repository is **public**.

**Tidying up:** the downloaded file is kept only in the app's private cache folder (one file, never in your Downloads,
gallery or files) and is named with its version, e.g. `TimeKillerGames-v2.4.3.apk`.
* Android's "allow installs from this app" switch is checked **before** anything is downloaded, so you are never made to download twice because of it.
* **Version cross-check:** every time the app starts, and every time it comes back on screen, it reads the version number
  out of each downloaded APK (not just the name) and compares it with the version that is installed. A file is deleted if it is
  the version you already have, an older version, a different app, damaged, or unfinished. Only a file that is being installed
  right now is kept. A newer file that was never installed is also removed (the app downloads a fresh one if you ask again).
* The home screen shows how much space update files take, and **Details** lists each file with the reason it is (not) needed,
  what the last tidy-up removed, and a **Delete all** button.
* These files are in the app's *private* storage (`/data/data/com.neil1454.drawsomething/cache`), which Android does not let file
  managers open. That is by design. Check it from Settings → Apps → this app → Storage: the Cache size should be tiny, and
  "Clear cache" empties it. Samsung's My Files "APK files" view only lists APKs in shared folders, so ours never appear there.

## Code layout

```
app/src/main/java/com/neil1454/drawsomething/
  core/   pure Kotlin game logic: engine, scoring, words, messages, drawing board (JVM-testable)

  GameViewModel.kt, MainActivity.kt
app/src/test/   unit tests, including full two-player games played through the real wire format
```

The drawer's phone is the referee for its round (word, clock, verdict, score); the guesser's
phone just displays what it's told. The secret word is never sent to the guesser until the
round ends. Drawings are sent as normalised (0–1) strokes, so a phone and a tablet show the
identical picture.
